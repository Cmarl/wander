# Wander — world brainstorm

A world that never feels like you've found everything.

Status: thinking only. Nothing here is built. Read-only pass.

---

## 1. Where the world actually is

Grounded in the current build rather than memory:

| | |
|---|---|
| Landmark kinds | **6** — lookout, arch, mast, spring, lone tree, wreck |
| Landmark grid | 2.2 km cells at 54% occupancy → ~2.2 km mean spacing |
| Name grammar | 38 leads × 17 middles × 6 kinds ≈ **3,900 possible names** |
| Prop kinds | 5 — pine, broadleaf, dead, shrub, rock |
| Wildlife | 8 species, habitat-gated |
| Biomes | 13 named surfaces/biomes |
| Bundle | 624 KB, zero assets, $0 hosting |

So: a player who drives for two hours has seen **every landmark kind, probably several times each**, and every species. The names stay fresh — that part already works — but the *things* don't. That's the actual complaint underneath "we should strive to feel like we have never found everything."

---

## 2. The thesis: stop adding nouns, add grammar

The obvious move is more kinds. Six becomes twenty, and the problem comes back in five hours instead of two. **You cannot out-run exhaustion by addition.** Every noun you add is consumed exactly once.

What doesn't get consumed is **combination**. A landmark kind is a noun; what a player actually remembers is a *sentence*:

> "the burnt-out lookout above Ashfall, with somebody's woodpile still stacked under it"

That sentence is four hashes deep — kind, variant, condition, accretion — and the space it's drawn from is multiplicative. Six kinds × four structural variants × four weathering states × a handful of optional satellite details is already **several thousand distinct instances**, and every one of them is still only six models' worth of work.

So the spine of this whole pass:

> **Variation should be multiplicative, not additive. Add a few nouns; add a lot of grammar.**

### The density tension, honestly

The game-feel brainstorm said *don't fill every valley*. Clyde now says *never found everything*. These sound opposed. They aren't — they're different axes, and conflating them is how calm open worlds turn into checklists.

- *Don't fill every valley* is about **local density** — how much stuff there is per kilometre. This should stay low. The emptiness is the product; it's what makes arriving mean anything.
- *Never found everything* is about **global variety** — how large the space of possible things is. This should be enormous.

You want **sparse and inexhaustible**, not dense and finite. Concretely: keep landmark spacing at ~2.2 km, and spend all the new effort on making each one less predictable. The failure mode to avoid is *content density*; the lever to pull is **identity density** — more uniqueness per thing, not more things per valley.

## 3. Variation within what already exists

The highest value work, and none of it needs a new model.

### 3.1 Variants — `M`

Each kind gets 3–5 structural variants chosen from the cell hash. Not reskins: different builds.

| Kind | Variants |
|---|---|
| Lookout | intact · burnt out (charred legs, no cabin) · collapsed (deck on the ground) · lived in (washing line, stovepipe, woodpile) |
| Arch | single span · double span · collapsed keystone (two pillars, no arch) · sea arch at the shore |
| Mast | guyed lattice · stub with the top sheared off · relay hut at the base · toppled, lying across the slope |
| Spring | open pool · terraced flats · geyser (periodic plume) · frozen over, in the cold |
| Lone tree | broad oak · wind-sculpted (all branches one way) · lightning-split · dead and bleached |
| Wreck | truck · bus · caravan · trailer with its load still strapped on |

**Why it hooks:** cresting a ridge and seeing a lookout you've seen twenty times, except this one has *burned*, is a stronger moment than a twenty-first kind of structure would be.

### 3.2 Weathering — `S`

One extra axis across everything: **pristine → weathered → ruined → overgrown**. Drives material colour, which layers render at all (a ruined lookout drops its cabin layer), and how much scrub the scatter puts around the base. Four states on top of variants is a 4× multiplier for a day's work, because the layer system already exists.

### 3.3 Accretions — `S`–`M`

Small satellite objects hashed around a landmark, each present or absent independently: woodpile, firepit, cairn, signpost, washing line, fuel drums, a parked wreck, a dog kennel, a grave marker.

Five optional accretions is **32 combinations per landmark**, from one small kit. This is the cheapest multiplier available and the one that most makes a place feel *used*.

### 3.4 Pairing — `M`

Occasionally two landmarks relate: a mast *above* a settlement, a wreck *at* an arch, a cairn trail *leading to* a lookout. Generated by letting a cell see its neighbours before deciding. Turns isolated objects into little stories.

## 4. New kinds actually worth adding — `M` each

Grammar first, but the noun list is genuinely thin at six. These earn their place because each unlocks a *different silhouette* or a *different biome*:

| Kind | Where | Why it earns a slot |
|---|---|---|
| **Shipwreck** | shoreline | Cut once already; shorelines have nothing of their own |
| **Standing stones** | steppe, meadow | Human but ancient — a different register from "someone built this" |
| **Mine adit** | scree, hills | A hole you can stand in; the only "interior" at landmark scale |
| **Windpump** | steppe | Moves. A turning thing visible for miles is magnetic |
| **Sinkhole** | arid | Negative space — everything else is additive |
| **Bone field** | shoreline, tundra | Whale ribs. Unmistakable at distance, slightly eerie |
| **Old orchard** | meadow | Rows of trees. The first sign of farming, and it reads instantly as *someone planted this* |
| **Frozen lake** | tundra, snow | A flat mirror in a world of slopes; conditional on cold |

That's 14 kinds. Combined with §3, the instance space goes from ~6 to **well over ten thousand**.

## 5. Buildings and settlements

### 5.1 Steadings — `M`

Before towns: the **single building**. A cabin, a barn, a boathouse, a chapel, a line shack. Placed on the existing landmark grid as just another kind, with variants and weathering.

This is the right first step — it tests the whole building kit at low risk, and a lone cabin on a far slope is already one of the most evocative things a landscape can offer.

### 5.2 Settlements — `L`

Rare and mostly empty. Placed on a **much coarser grid — 15–20 km** — with strict suitability: flat, low, sheltered, near water, temperate. The same logic that chose real sites, which means towns end up where they *look like* they belong.

Generation:
1. A **spine** — a short track following the local contour
2. **Buildings** placed along it from a kit of ~8 forms (cabin, barn, store, chapel, water tower, silo, pier, workshop), each hashed for variant and weathering
3. **Yards** between them: fences, a well, a vehicle, stacked timber
4. A **name** from an extended grammar — "Ashfall Crossing", "Windward Landing"

**Keep them small.** Four to nine buildings. A hamlet you can walk across in two minutes is a discovery; a city is a content-production problem and would swallow the project.

**Why rare matters:** at 15–20 km you'll find one every twenty-odd minutes of driving. That's an *event*. At 3 km it's scenery, and the emptiness that makes the rest of the world work is gone.

### 5.3 Shelter & Stay — `M`

The payoff for reaching a building. One interior room, no loading — the camera moves inside and the walls are simply there.

- **Shelter** — step inside, the wind drops, the audio goes close and muffled. Possibly the single most *relaxing* moment available, and it costs one audio duck and a camera move.
- **Stay the night** — camp's rest, indoors, with a different wake-up.
- Buildings each get their own verb per the features system: chapel → **sit**, water tower → **climb**, store → **take something** (a cosmetic), workshop → **repair** (pops the dents out of the truck).

### 5.4 Ways — `L`

Dirt tracks between settlements, generated as low-gradient paths between hashed endpoints. Rendered as a terrain-shader tint plus flattened grass plus wheel ruts.

**Why this is the big one:** a track answers a question the world currently can't — *where does this go?* Following a road is a completely different pleasure from crossing open country, and it costs nothing in content because the road generates itself. It also makes settlements findable without a map marker: follow the track.

**The risk, stated plainly:** roads could turn trackless wandering into route-following. Mitigation — keep them **sparse, faint and discontinuous**. They fade out across rock and snow and resume later. They're a hint, not a network.

## 6. People

Clyde asked about NPCs. My honest answer is that **animate NPCs are the wrong shape for this game**, and I'd push back before building any.

A person who talks needs dialogue, a schedule, and somewhere to be. All three create obligation: a conversation is something you must finish, a shopkeeper is somewhere you must return to, a quest-giver is a debt. The phone-down test fails immediately — putting the phone down mid-conversation is a loose end, and loose ends are precisely the anxiety this game is built to avoid. The uncanny cost is real too: a person who isn't quite a person is far more jarring than no person at all.

What I'd build instead, in order of value:

### 6.1 Traces — `S`–`M`
The evidence of people, with the people just out of frame. **This gets you 90% of what NPCs are for, at 10% of the cost and none of the obligation.**

Smoke from a chimney in a building you'll find empty · washing on a line · a firepit still warm · boot prints leading away · tyre tracks that end at nothing · a half-stacked woodpile · a kettle on a stove · a door left open, swinging.

### 6.2 The Dog — `M`
A dog that joins you at a steading and rides in the truck. No dialogue, no needs, no fail state. It sits in the passenger seat, gets out when you get out, noses around, comes back. You can name it — that name goes in the journal.

**This is the warmest thing on the entire list** and it carries zero social pressure, because a dog asks nothing of you.

### 6.3 The Other Wanderer — `M`
One single traveller in the whole world. Occasionally you crest a ridge and there's a truck on the far slope going the other way. They flash their lights. They're gone. No dialogue, no name, no meeting. Rare enough to be a story you tell.

### 6.4 Figures — `S`
A silhouette on a distant ridge that isn't there when you arrive. Pure atmosphere, no systems. Use sparingly, or it becomes a mechanic instead of a feeling.

## 7. Keeping it unfound

Variation makes instances unique. These make the *world* feel unexhausted.

### 7.1 Regions — `M`
A coarse layer (~50 km) giving each broad area a **character and a name**: which landmark kinds are common, which species, a palette bias, a weather bias. "The Ashlands", "The Long Coast", "Winterreach".

**This is the keystone.** Without it, more variation is just more noise. With it, travel becomes *going somewhere* — the land changes character over an hour's drive, and a region you haven't been to is a genuinely different place rather than the same generator with different dice.

### 7.2 Rarity tiers — `S`
Common / uncommon / rare / **once per region**. The rarest things should be scarce *per region*, not per session, so they cannot be farmed by restarting or grinding — you find one because you went somewhere, not because you looked harder.

### 7.3 Conditional finds — `S`–`M`
Things that exist only under conditions you rarely meet: a geyser that only plumes on the hour · a causeway only crossable at certain in-game times · an aurora only on certain real dates (Today's Sky already does this) · a frozen lake only in the coldest region · something visible only in fog.

**Why it hooks:** you can be in the right place and still not have found it. That's the cleanest possible answer to "I've seen everything."

### 7.4 Rumours — `M`
Noticeboards at settlements, and the radio masts, give you a **description and a direction, never a marker**:

> *"pale stones on a bald ridge, two days west of the Long Coast"*

Generated from a real landmark that genuinely exists at a real coordinate. It creates a goal without a quest marker, it's unbounded, and it ties straight into the existing pin/glassing loop — you go *look* for it.

It also feeds the share-link loop: a rumour can carry a real `?at=` coordinate, so a noticeboard is, structurally, the same thing as a postcard.

### 7.5 The Register — `M`
The journal, grouped by **region** rather than by time, reading as a travelogue. Still no totals, no percentages, no empty slots — but seeing *"The Long Coast — 7 places"* next to *"Winterreach — nothing yet"* is the gentlest possible invitation to go somewhere new. It implies more world without ever listing what's missing.

## 8. What I'd build, in order

| # | Thing | Effort | Why this one now |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Regions** (7.1) | M | The keystone. Every other idea here is better if the world has named character, and worse if it doesn't. |
| 2 | **Variants + Weathering** (3.1, 3.2) | M | The biggest freshness-per-hour on the list. Multiplies all existing content without a single new model. |
| 3 | **Accretions** (3.3) | S–M | Cheapest multiplier available, and what most makes a place feel used rather than placed. |
| 4 | **Steadings** (5.1) | M | A lone cabin on a far slope. Tests the whole building kit at low risk before committing to towns. |
| 5 | **Traces of people** (6.1) | S–M | 90% of what NPCs are for, none of the obligation. Pairs with steadings. |
| 6 | **New landmark kinds** (4) | M | Six is genuinely thin; shoreline and arid country have nothing of their own. |
| 7 | **Settlements** (5.2) | L | The headline ask. Needs 4 and 1 to exist first or it's a kit with no grammar. |
| 8 | **Shelter & Stay** (5.3) | M | The payoff for reaching a building, and possibly the most relaxing moment in the game. |
| 9 | **Rumours + Register** (7.4, 7.5) | M | Turns a bigger world into a reason to keep looking. Needs a bigger world first. |
| 10 | **The Dog** (6.2) | M | The warmest thing on the list. Could honestly be built any time. |

Then: Ways, conditional finds, the Other Wanderer, pairing.

**Stages 1–5 are the real milestone.** That combination means every landmark you meet is a different instance, in a named region, with somebody's woodpile beside it and smoke on the next ridge — without a single town built yet.

## 9. Anti-goals

- **No dialogue, ever.** The moment someone talks, the game owes you a conversation.
- **No settlement bigger than a hamlet.** Cities are a content-production problem that would consume the project and deliver a worse game.
- **No quest log, no markers you didn't place.** Rumours describe; they never point.
- **No region completion UI.** "7 places found in the Long Coast" is fine. "7/12" is a checklist and poisons the whole thing.
- **No economy, no currency, no trading.** Carries obligation and invites optimisation.
- **Don't raise landmark density.** All of this works at the current ~2.2 km spacing. Raising it would trade the emptiness for content, which is exactly the wrong trade.
- **Don't break the zero-asset rule.** Everything here is generatable; none of it needs a model file.

## Q:

**Q: How rare should settlements be?** This decides whether a town is an event or scenery, and it's hard to change later once the world "feels" a certain density.
*Recommendation: very rare — a 15–20 km grid with strict suitability, so roughly one per twenty minutes of driving, four to nine buildings each. Rare and small beats frequent and large for this game, every time.*

**Q: NPCs — do you want actual people?** You asked for them; I'd argue against animate ones, and I'd rather be overruled explicitly than quietly substitute something else.
*Recommendation: no talking NPCs. Build Traces, a dog, and one rare Other Wanderer instead. If after playing that it still feels unpeopled, the cheapest next step is a single silent figure who waves — not a conversation.*

**Q: Do roads belong in a trackless wandering game?** Ways are the single biggest world-feel idea here and also the one most able to change the game's character — from "go anywhere" to "follow the route".
*Recommendation: yes, but faint, sparse and discontinuous — fading out over rock and snow and resuming later. A hint, not a network. Build it after settlements so the roads have somewhere to go.*

**Q: Should we go inside buildings?** Interiors are a real scope decision — one room with no loading is cheap, anything more is a different project.
*Recommendation: one room, no loading, no furniture systems. Shelter from weather and a place to sleep is the entire feature, and it's probably the most relaxing thirty seconds the game could offer.*

**Q: Is "never found everything" worth any loss of emptiness?** Everything above is designed to need none — but it's your call whether you'd trade some quiet for more to find.
*Recommendation: trade none of it. Keep spacing exactly where it is and spend every bit of effort on making each thing less predictable. Sparse and inexhaustible is a better game than dense and finite, and it's also far cheaper to build.*
